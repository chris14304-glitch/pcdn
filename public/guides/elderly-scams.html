Elderly Scam Safety: Recognizing and Preventing Fraud Against Older Adults

Older Americans lose billions of dollars each year to fraud. This guide covers the most common scams targeting seniors, how each one works, and the practical steps that protect against them—written for older adults and for the family members who help them.

Why Scammers Target Older Adults

According to the FBI's Elder Fraud Report, Americans over 60 lost more than three billion dollars to fraud in a single recent year, and the true number is likely far higher because a large share of victims never report the crime. Older adults are targeted for specific, cynical reasons.

Many people over 60 have accumulated a lifetime of savings, own their homes outright, and have access to retirement accounts. They are more likely to answer landline calls, take the caller at their word, and be at home when calls arrive during business hours. Many grew up in an era when banks, government agencies, and utility companies did not lie to their customers. That default trust, which was reasonable in 1975, is dangerous in 2026.

Being scammed is not a sign of diminished intelligence. Career law enforcement officers, retired accountants, and former corporate executives are all victims in the FBI's case files. Modern scams are engineered by organized professional operations, often overseas, that run thousands of attempts a day and refine their scripts based on what works. When they hit the right person at the right vulnerable moment, almost anyone can be convinced.

Key Takeaway

Shame is what keeps most fraud invisible. If a scam succeeds, the victim often stays silent and the same criminals return for more money. Talking openly about scams—the ones you saw, the ones that almost worked—is one of the strongest defenses a community has.

The Most Common Scams

The specific stories change, but the categories are stable. Learning to recognize the category matters more than memorizing every variation.

  • Tech support scams — a pop-up or call claims your computer is infected and asks for remote access
  • Grandparent scams — a call claims a grandchild is in trouble and needs money now
  • Government impersonation — "IRS," "Social Security," or "Medicare" threatens arrest or benefit loss
  • Bank and card fraud calls — "your bank" reports suspicious activity and needs you to move money to a "safe account"
  • Romance scams — a long-distance online relationship escalates into requests for money
  • Sweepstakes and lottery — you have "won" a prize but must pay taxes or fees first
  • Utility shutoff — a caller threatens to cut power or water unless you pay immediately
  • Medicare / health insurance — a caller offers a "new card" or free medical equipment in exchange for your Medicare number
  • Home repair and contractor fraud — an unsolicited worker offers to fix your roof, driveway, or trees for cash upfront
  • Fake charity appeals — especially after natural disasters or during the holidays
  • Investment and "safe" cryptocurrency schemes — a friendly voice or online contact promises high returns with no risk

Tech Support Scams

Tech support fraud is one of the highest-loss categories reported by older victims. It typically begins in one of two ways.

How It Starts

In the first version, you are browsing the web and a full-screen alert appears, often with a loud siren or robotic voice, claiming that your computer is infected, that Microsoft has locked your device, or that your bank information has been stolen. A phone number is displayed to call for "immediate assistance."

In the second version, the phone rings first. The caller claims to be from Microsoft, Apple, Norton, McAfee, or your internet provider. They say they have detected a problem with your computer and need to fix it right away.

What Happens Next

The person on the phone asks you to install remote-access software (common names include AnyDesk, TeamViewer, LogMeIn, or "Support Assistant"). Once they have control of your screen, they show you convincingly scary technical output—a command prompt full of red text, a "virus scan" identifying threats—that would look alarming to anyone. They then explain that fixing it requires paying for a support plan, security software, or, eventually, moving money out of your bank accounts to protect them.

In more elaborate versions, they log into your online banking while controlling your screen, "accidentally" deposit far too much money in a test transfer, and then panic—telling you they'll be fired unless you help by sending the "excess" back via gift cards, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency. There was no test transfer. The number they showed you was fake. The money you send is gone.

Ground Rules
  • Microsoft, Apple, Google, and your ISP will never call you unprompted about a computer problem
  • Never allow remote access to your computer from an incoming call or pop-up
  • Never open your banking apps or websites while someone else is watching your screen
  • If a pop-up locks your browser, close the browser or restart the computer—do not call the number

Grandparent and Impostor Scams

The classic version: the phone rings, a young voice on the other end says "Grandma? It's me!" You supply the grandchild's name yourself—"Michael?"—and now the scammer knows exactly who to be. Michael is in jail, or in a car accident, or stranded overseas, and needs money wired urgently. He begs you not to tell his parents.

Modern versions are more sophisticated. Attackers can now use AI voice cloning to reproduce a grandchild's voice from a few seconds of audio scraped from social media. The person crying on the phone sounds exactly like the person you love. In some documented cases, a second voice (the "lawyer" or "police officer") takes over to add authority and instruct the victim on how to send the money.

The Defense

  • Hang up and call back. Call the grandchild's actual number, or their parents, on a phone number you already have—not one the caller gave you.
  • Set a family code word. Agree in advance on a word or phrase that only real family members know. If someone calls in crisis and cannot supply it, it is a scam.
  • No family member in real trouble needs gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency. These payment demands are the giveaway, always.
  • Real lawyers and police do not call grandparents demanding immediate cash to release a family member. Bail is paid through a court system, not through a courier picking up money at your front door.

Government and Bank Impersonation

Impostors of the IRS, Social Security Administration, Medicare, U.S. Marshals, and your own bank are all part of the same fraud ecosystem. They use fear—of arrest, of losing benefits, of an emptied account—to override your judgment and force you to act before you can think.

What They Say

  • "There is a warrant out for your arrest for unpaid taxes."
  • "Your Social Security number has been suspended due to suspicious activity."
  • "Your Medicare card is being reissued—please confirm your number."
  • "We detected suspicious charges. To protect your money we need to move it to a secure account."
  • "A drug package with your name on it was intercepted at the border."

What Is Actually True

  • The IRS initiates contact by physical mail. They do not call, text, or email you demanding immediate payment.
  • The Social Security Administration does not "suspend" numbers. Numbers are permanent.
  • Medicare does not call to sell you a plan or "reissue" a card that requires you to give your number.
  • Your bank will never ask you to move money out of your own account to a "safe account." That request is the scam. Full stop.
  • No federal agency accepts payment in gift cards, wire transfers to individuals, or cryptocurrency.
Caller ID Lies

Modern phone systems make it trivial for a scammer to display any name or number they choose. A call that appears to come from the IRS, your bank, or even your local police station can be spoofed. Never treat caller ID as proof of who is calling. Always hang up and call back on a number you look up yourself.

Romance Scams

Romance scams cause more emotional damage than almost any other category. They typically begin on dating sites, Facebook, or Words With Friends and similar games. The "match" is charming, attentive, patient, and—critically—always has a reason they cannot meet in person or video call. They are on a military deployment, on an oil rig, working overseas as a doctor, or building a business that is about to close a major deal.

The relationship progresses over weeks or months. Real emotional connection develops. Then a crisis appears: a stuck inheritance, a medical emergency, a customs fee, a business emergency. Only you can help. Later, an "investment opportunity" appears, often in cryptocurrency, where they promise to guide you through making enormous returns. Every dollar sent disappears into a fictional life.

Warning Signs

  • Moves the conversation off the dating site quickly to text, WhatsApp, or Telegram
  • Professes strong feelings very early
  • Consistently avoids video calls, or the "video calls" are frozen/blurry
  • The profile photos look like a model or match reverse-image results for someone else
  • A crisis emerges that only money can solve
  • An "amazing investment opportunity" is introduced, usually involving cryptocurrency
  • Encourages secrecy from family and friends

Universal Red Flags

Almost every scam, regardless of the story, contains most of these ingredients. When you recognize the pattern, the specific story stops mattering.

  • Unexpected contact. You did not initiate this call, email, text, or message.
  • Urgency. Action must be taken today, this hour, this minute. There is no time to check.
  • Fear or excitement. Either something terrible will happen or something wonderful will—both are designed to stop clear thinking.
  • Secrecy. Do not tell your family, your bank, the police, or anyone else.
  • Unusual payment methods. Gift cards, wire transfers to individuals, cryptocurrency, or cash couriers.
  • Requests for verification codes. A six-digit code sent to your phone that you are asked to read to the caller.
  • Remote access. Any request to install software so they can "look at your computer."
  • Escalating scripts. The moment you hesitate, another "supervisor" or "officer" takes over.

The Payment Methods Scammers Demand

Legitimate businesses and government agencies use ordinary payment methods that can be traced and reversed. Scammers demand the ones that cannot.

Gift Cards

Google Play, Apple, Steam, Target, Amazon, and Walmart cards are all popular. Once you read the numbers on the back of a gift card to someone, the money is gone within minutes and cannot be recovered. No legitimate entity—no government agency, no utility, no bank, no employer, no relative in trouble—is ever paid in gift cards.

Wire Transfers

Wire transfers via Western Union, MoneyGram, or your bank are effectively irreversible once the recipient picks up the funds. Scammers often instruct you to lie to the wire clerk about the reason for the transfer.

Cryptocurrency

Bitcoin ATMs are increasingly appearing in convenience stores. Scammers walk victims through the exact steps: withdraw cash, drive to a specific machine, scan the QR code they send you, insert the cash. There is no way to reverse a completed cryptocurrency transaction.

Peer-to-Peer Apps

Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, and PayPal "Friends and Family" transfers are treated as personal payments and, once completed, are essentially impossible to reverse. Banks are increasingly firm that they will not refund scam losses on these platforms.

Cash Couriers

Scammers sometimes send a "courier" to pick up envelopes of cash from your home. This is common in grandparent scams and government impostor cases. Never give cash to a stranger at your door, no matter who they claim to be sent by.

The Golden Rule

The moment anyone asks you to pay in gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfer, or cash to a courier, the interaction is a scam. There is no exception. Stop, hang up, and call a family member.

Protecting Yourself and Your Household

You do not need to become paranoid or stop enjoying the phone and the internet. A small number of habits blocks the vast majority of attempted fraud.

For Your Phone

  • Let unknown numbers go to voicemail. Legitimate callers leave messages.
  • Register on the National Do Not Call Registry at donotcall.gov — will not stop scammers, but reduces legitimate telemarketing so illegitimate calls stand out.
  • Ask your phone carrier about their spam-blocking service (AT&T ActiveArmor, Verizon Call Filter, T-Mobile Scam Shield—many are free).
  • If a caller pressures or frightens you, hang up. It is always acceptable to hang up.

For Your Bank Accounts

  • Set up transaction alerts on every account so you see charges in real time.
  • Consider adding a "trusted contact" to your bank and brokerage accounts—a family member the institution can call if unusual activity is detected.
  • Ask your bank about a "large transaction hold" that requires an in-person confirmation for withdrawals over a threshold.
  • Never share online banking passwords or verification codes with anyone, including someone claiming to be from the bank.

For Your Computer

  • Keep the operating system and web browser updated.
  • Do not install software that a phone caller or pop-up tells you to install.
  • If a pop-up locks your browser, hold down the power button to shut the computer off, then start it fresh.
  • Have a family member or trusted local shop help you check your machine every few months.

For Your Front Door and Mailbox

  • Get a locked mailbox or a P.O. box if mail theft is a concern in your area.
  • Shred anything with account numbers, Social Security numbers, or Medicare numbers before discarding.
  • Do not sign contracts with door-to-door contractors on the spot. Ask for a written estimate, verify the license, and get a second quote.

How Family Members Can Help

If you are an adult child or friend of an older adult, your role is not to take away independence. It is to build a system where a suspicious situation becomes a normal thing to talk through.

  • Have the conversation before it is needed. Bring up recent scam news at dinner. Share stories you have read. Make it a normal topic.
  • Be the "check with someone" person. Explicitly say: "If anything comes up that sounds urgent or scary or too good to be true, call me first. Any time of day. I will never be annoyed."
  • Do not shame past mistakes. If a scam succeeded, the response should be practical help, not "I told you so." Shame keeps future scams hidden.
  • Consider financial safeguards together. Alerts, trusted contacts, and joint account monitoring can be added without taking over control.
  • Watch for warning signs of exploitation: unusual large withdrawals, new "friends" involved in finances, secrecy about mail or calls, missing valuables, sudden changes to estate documents.
  • Set up code words for family emergency calls, so an AI-cloned voice cannot succeed.
The Cognitive Decline Question

Repeated victimization can be an early warning sign of cognitive decline. If a parent falls for the same category of scam twice, or seems unable to remember conversations about a specific fraud attempt, talk with their physician. Early intervention protects both their money and their independence.

If You Have Already Been Scammed

First: you are not alone, and you are not stupid. Millions of Americans have been through exactly what you are going through. What matters now is acting quickly to limit damage and to help law enforcement catch the people responsible.

  1. Stop all contact with the scammer. Do not answer further calls. Do not respond to threats. Do not send more money to "recover" what was already sent—that is the recovery-scam variant, and it is run by the same criminals.
  2. Contact your bank immediately. If a wire, Zelle, or check was sent recently, the bank may be able to recall it. Time matters—within 24 to 48 hours is the best chance.
  3. Report to your credit card companies if cards were used. Dispute the charges as fraud.
  4. Change passwords on any account the scammer may have accessed, especially email and online banking. Enable two-factor authentication.
  5. Report the fraud to the agencies listed in the next section. Reports feed law enforcement investigations even when your specific case is not solved.
  6. Consider a credit freeze at all three credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) if personal identifying information was shared. This is free and prevents new accounts from being opened in your name.
  7. Talk to someone. A trusted family member, your doctor, or a fraud victim support line. The emotional impact of being scammed is real and lasting; you do not have to carry it alone.

Where to Report and Get Help

  • FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) — ic3.gov — the primary federal reporting site for online fraud, including elder fraud
  • FTC Fraud Report — reportfraud.ftc.gov — feeds the federal fraud database used by law enforcement
  • National Elder Fraud Hotline — 1-833-372-8311 — Department of Justice line staffed by case managers who help victims report to the right agencies and access support
  • AARP Fraud Watch Helpline — 1-877-908-3360 — free help for anyone (not just AARP members) who has been targeted or victimized
  • State Attorney General's Office — each state has a consumer protection division that investigates fraud reported by residents
  • Adult Protective Services — if an older adult in your life is being financially exploited, especially by a caregiver or family member, contact your state's APS office
  • Credit bureaus — Equifax (1-800-685-1111), Experian (1-888-397-3742), TransUnion (1-888-909-8872) — to place fraud alerts or credit freezes
A Simple Anti-Scam Checklist
  • Let unknown numbers go to voicemail; never trust caller ID
  • No legitimate caller ever asks for gift cards, cryptocurrency, or wire transfers
  • Government agencies do not threaten arrest by phone
  • Your bank will never ask you to move money to a "safe account"
  • Never allow remote access to your computer from a call or pop-up
  • Set a family code word for emergency calls involving loved ones
  • Set up transaction alerts and a trusted contact on your bank accounts
  • When anything feels urgent or off, hang up and call a family member
  • Talk about scams openly—silence protects the scammers, not you

Scammers rely on isolation, embarrassment, and speed. Every conversation you have with a family member, neighbor, or friend about a scam you noticed—whether or not you almost fell for it—chips away at the tools they depend on. Awareness spreads faster than fraud when people are willing to talk. If this guide helps you or someone you love avoid a single loss, or recover from one that already happened, it has done its job.
